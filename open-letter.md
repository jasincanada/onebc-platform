# Cancel should be as easy as subscribe

**An open letter to every federal, provincial and territorial political party in Canada**  
**Jasminder Bangar · Surrey, British Columbia · 1 October 2026**

Subscription fatigue is no longer a minor annoyance. It is an affordability problem, a consumer-autonomy problem and an enforcement problem.

The request is simple: put enforceable subscription fairness in your platform. Require informed consent, advance renewal notice, cancellation through the same channel used to subscribe, prompt refunds, accessible support and regulators that can act on patterns before thousands of people are harmed.

Canadians are being asked to rent more and own less: entertainment, software, news, cloud storage, delivery, fitness, telecommunications and increasingly the tools used for work and education. Each charge may look manageable in isolation. Together they become a permanent layer of household overhead, scattered across cards, app stores and bank accounts.

The burden is not only the price. It is the time required to remember every renewal, locate the right account, understand changing terms, find the cancellation control, preserve evidence and fight for a refund when a company’s own promise is not honoured. A subscription economy that relies on people forgetting, giving up or lacking the capacity to escalate is not convenient. It is a transfer of risk from businesses to households.

## The pressure is measurable

Statistics Canada reports that Canadian households spent $11.3 billion on cable, satellite and other program-distribution services, including streaming, in 2024. Prices for video and audio subscription services were 21% higher than in 2019 and 52.7% higher than in 2014. In May 2025, those prices were up another 5.4% year over year. [Statistics Canada](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/o1/en/plus/8248-medium-cheaper-message-pricier)

A 2024 Hardbacon online survey of 516 Canadian adults found that respondents had an average of eight recurring subscriptions while initially believing they had four; 66% said they had paid for a subscription they had forgotten, and 73% had taken a free trial or promotional price intending to cancel but failed to do so in time. The sample is modest and the findings should not be treated as official population estimates, but the gap between perceived and actual commitments captures the design problem. [Survey release and methodology](https://www.newswire.ca/news-releases/survey-73-of-canadians-trapped-by-recurring-subscriptions-824802603.html)

Roku’s industry-sponsored 2026 survey of 2,043 Canadian adults found that regular viewers spent an average of 13 minutes searching for something to watch; for daily viewers, that approaches 80 hours a year. Six in 10 said they could not always remember which app carried the title they wanted. [Roku study](https://newsroom.roku.com/news/2026/09/overwhelmed-by-choice-canadians-spend-about-80-hours-a/ubmyqakn-1790614194)

> Subscription fatigue is what happens when recurring payment becomes the default, attention becomes the enforcement mechanism, and cancellation becomes unpaid work.

## One complaint shows the gap

In April 2026, my Instacart+ annual membership auto-renewed for $54.88. Instacart support gave me a written commitment that the fee would be refunded if I cancelled within five days. I relied on that representation and cancelled within the stated window. The company then said the membership was ineligible for a refund. Only after repeated escalation and screenshots of its own commitment did it reverse course, issue the refund and describe it as a one-time courtesy rather than performance of the promise already made.

I reported the episode to Consumer Protection BC not to recover more money, but to document a pattern: automatic renewal, contradictory representations, friction around cancellation and a support process that required unusual persistence and technical literacy.

Consumer Protection BC first replied that the issue was outside the laws and mandates it oversees and directed me toward private dispute-resolution options. After I asked it to identify the provisions it had considered under the *Business Practices and Consumer Protection Act*, it said it would not open a complaint at that time, would document the complaint, might monitor the situation, and would reassess whether an investigation was necessary if more complaints arose.

That response did not decide that the representation was truthful, the process fair or the conduct harmless. It showed how enforcement can depend on enough consumers independently overcoming the same friction, assembling evidence and filing complaints before a pattern becomes actionable. People with the least time, money, health, language access or digital confidence are the least able to create that record.

## British Columbia moved

British Columbia’s stronger subscription rules took effect on August 1, 2026. For renewals longer than 60 days, businesses must permit cancellation before or after renewal, provide notice 30 to 60 days before renewal, explain how to cancel and issue an eligible prorated refund within 15 days. For renewal terms of 60 days or less, consumers must be able to cancel before or after renewal without a fee or penalty. [Current B.C. statute](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/04002_00) · [Consumer guidance](https://www.consumerprotectionbc.ca/consumer-help/subscription-contracts/)

That is meaningful progress. It also proves that clearer rules are possible. But a Canadian’s basic protection should not depend on a postal code, whether a term is 59 or 61 days, which payment intermediary processed it, or whether the supplier is based in another province or country.

Federal authorities already recognize the mechanism. Canada’s Office of Consumer Affairs lists intentionally difficult cancellation and automatic opt-ins among dark commercial patterns. The Competition Bureau and Office of the Privacy Commissioner say obstructive design can make it difficult to cancel or switch services and that delayed or complicated information can create a misleading general impression. [Office of Consumer Affairs](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/office-consumer-affairs/en/dark-patterns) · [Competition Bureau and Privacy Commissioner](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/how-we-foster-competition/collaboration-and-partnerships/digital-design-support-informed-consumer-choices)

## The standard Canada needs

1. **Affirmative consent.** Recurring charges must require clear, separate and informed agreement.
2. **Renewal reminders that work.** Businesses must send prominent notice before every material renewal or price increase.
3. **Equal cancellation.** If a person can subscribe online in minutes, that person must be able to cancel online in no more steps.
4. **Confirmation and refunds.** Cancellation must generate an immediate timestamped receipt and prompt prorated refunds where required.
5. **No interface coercion.** Ban misleading buttons, hidden controls, repeated nagging, confirm-shaming and false urgency.
6. **Accessibility by design.** Processes must work for disabled people, seniors, people with limited literacy and people who cannot use voice calls.
7. **A national floor.** Governments should create interoperable minimum rights while allowing stronger provincial and territorial protection.
8. **Enforcement before exhaustion.** Regulators should aggregate complaints, publish screening reasons and test cancellation journeys.
9. **Real consequences.** Remedies should include restitution, proportionate penalties, corrective notices and enforceable compliance orders.

## A test for every party

Every party should answer the same five questions:

- Will you require cancellation to be as easy as sign-up?
- Will you require clear advance notice and affirmative consent for recurring charges and material price changes?
- Will you guarantee prompt refunds for unlawful or improperly disclosed renewals?
- Will you fund regulators to investigate patterns instead of sending each consumer to private litigation?
- Will you publish a timetable for federal–provincial–territorial coordination on digital subscription rights?

## What leadership looks like

Leadership is not another page advising people to read the fine print, monitor every statement and keep every screenshot. Those are sensible survival tactics, but they cannot be the architecture of consumer protection. The business controls the interface, contract, billing system and data. The business should bear the duty to prove informed consent, proper notice and a functional exit.

The strongest policy will not prohibit subscriptions. It will make honest subscriptions compete on value rather than inertia. Businesses that provide continuing value will retain customers. Businesses that depend on confusion, forgetfulness or exhaustion will lose a subsidy they never deserved.

Canadians should not need a lawyer, a regulator, a public campaign and hours of persistence to stop a recurring charge. Put subscription fairness in your platform, introduce the legislation, resource its enforcement and publish the results.
